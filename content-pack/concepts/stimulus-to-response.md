## Detecting the stimulus

UV-B light is perceived by dimers of the UVR8 protein, probably through a tryptophan-based mechanism [12]. Absorbing UV-B splits the dimer and lets UVR8 interact with COP1, a central regulator of light signalling [12]. Bacteria are detected through their molecules: the flagellin receptor FLS2 is a putative receptor kinase [34].

One receptor can sense more than one thing. Phytochrome B, a red-light receptor, also takes part in temperature perception through its temperature-dependent reversion from the active to the inactive state [4]. Its inactivation rate in the dark is proportional to temperature, so phytochromes can integrate temperature over the course of the night [3].

## The cascade

When water is short, the hormone abscisic acid (ABA) binds PYR/PYL receptors, which then bind and inhibit type 2C protein phosphatases [66]. In guard cells the kinase OST1 activates the anion channel SLAC1, while the phosphatase ABI1 in the same complex prevents that activation [67]. ABA then closes the stomata [67].

## Carrying the signal through the plant

Plants have no nerves, yet a wounded leaf warns undamaged ones. Glutamate acts as a wound signal: ion channels of the GLUTAMATE RECEPTOR-LIKE family convert it into a rise in intracellular calcium that spreads to distant organs, where defences are switched on [28]. After wounding, electrical changes at the leaf surface match the domains of jasmonate signalling in undamaged leaves [29].
